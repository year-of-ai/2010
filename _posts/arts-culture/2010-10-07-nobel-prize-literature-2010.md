# Nobel Prize in Literature 2010

**Category:** Literature / Literary Awards

**Key figures:** Mario Vargas Llosa, Swedish Academy, Peter Englund (Permanent Secretary), Lars Lönnroth (former Academy Chair)

## Summary

On October 7, 2010, the Swedish Academy announced that Mario Vargas Llosa, the Peruvian-Spanish novelist, essayist, and political figure, had been awarded the 2010 Nobel Prize in Literature "for his cartography of the structures of power and his trenchant images of the individual's resistance, revolt and defeat." Born on March 28, 1936, in Arequipa, Peru, Vargas Llosa rose to international prominence through novels including *The Green House* (1966), *Conversation in the Cathedral* (1969), and *The War of the End of the World* (1981), which examined political violence, historical upheaval, and personal agency within Latin American contexts.

The prize carried a monetary award of 10 million Swedish kronor (~$1.5 million USD at 2010 exchange rates). The formal ceremony was held on December 10, 2010, in Stockholm's Concert Hall. In his Nobel Lecture, titled "In Praise of Reading and Fiction" ("Elogio de la lectura y la ficción"), Vargas Llosa argued that literature is an antidote to political totalitarianism: "Thanks to literature, to the consciousness it shapes, the desires and longings it inspires, and our disenchantment with reality when we return from the book's imaginary world, civilization is now less cruel than when storytellers began to humanize life with their fables."

The 2010 Prize was significant as the first awarded to a Peruvian author and the first to a Latin American writer since Octavio Paz in 1990. Vargas Llosa joined a lineage of Latin American laureates in Literature that included Gabriela Mistral (Chile, 1945), Miguel Ángel Asturias (Guatemala, 1967), Pablo Neruda (Chile, 1971), Gabriel García Márquez (Colombia, 1982), and Paz (Mexico, 1990). At 74, Vargas Llosa was among the older laureates at the time of award.

## Career and Key Works

Vargas Llosa's literary output spans more than 50 years and ranges across realism, experimental fiction, and political satire:

- **The Time of the Hero** (*La ciudad y los perros*, 1963): his debut novel, set in a Lima military school, drew on his own experience and was publicly burned by the Peruvian military upon publication, an act that paradoxically boosted its international visibility.
- **The Green House** (*La casa verde*, 1966): a structurally complex novel weaving together five narrative strands across Peru's jungle and desert settings; it won the Rómulo Gallegos Prize and the Premio de la Crítica.
- **Conversation in the Cathedral** (*Conversación en La Catedral*, 1969): widely considered his masterpiece, this 600-page novel traces political corruption in Peru during the Odría dictatorship (1948–1956) through four interleaved narratives.
- **Aunt Julia and the Scriptwriter** (*La tía Julia y el escribidor*, 1977): a comic, semi-autobiographical novel about a young law student who falls in love with his older aunt.
- **The War of the End of the World** (*La guerra del fin del mundo*, 1981): a historical novel about the Canudos conflict in 1890s Brazil, often compared to Tolstoy in its scale.
- **The Feast of the Goat** (*La fiesta del chivo*, 2000): a political thriller centered on the last days of Dominican dictator Rafael Trujillo, considered his most politically engaged mature work.

In the essays and non-fiction realm, Vargas Llosa's *A Writer's Reality* (1991) and *Making Waves* (1997) outlined his literary theory, while *The Call of the Tribe* (2018) traced his intellectual journey from Sartrean Marxism to Popperian liberalism.

## Political Career and Ideology

Vargas Llosa's public intellectual life made him as famous as his novels in Latin America and Spain. In 1987, he led massive public protests in Peru against the nationalization of banks proposed by President Alan García. In 1990, he ran for the Peruvian presidency on a liberal economic platform under the Frente Democrático (FREDEMO) coalition, losing in the runoff to the then-unknown Alberto Fujimori, who governed Peru until 2000 before fleeing corruption charges. Vargas Llosa subsequently adopted Spanish citizenship (while retaining Peruvian citizenship) and became an outspoken critic of authoritarian populism — Hugo Chávez's Venezuela in particular — positioning himself at odds with much of Latin America's literary left, including his former ally Gabriel García Márquez.

This ideological complexity — a novelist celebrated for depicting poverty and political oppression who politically advocated free markets — fueled ongoing debate about the relationship between aesthetic achievement and political commitment in literature.

## Context: The 2010 Nobel Prizes

The 2010 Nobel Prizes collectively marked an exceptional moment of scientific and cultural recognition:

- **Physics** (October 5): Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov for graphene isolation (University of Manchester, 2004).
- **Chemistry** (October 6): Richard Heck, Ei-ichi Negishi, and Akira Suzuki for palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions.
- **Physiology or Medicine** (October 4): Robert G. Edwards for in vitro fertilization.
- **Literature** (October 7): Mario Vargas Llosa.
- **Peace** (October 8): Liu Xiaobo — imprisoned Chinese dissident — accepted in absentia, with China's government condemning the award and blocking Chinese nationals from the ceremony.

The 2010 Peace Prize in particular became an international flashpoint: 19 countries (including China, Russia, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan) declined their invitations to the December ceremony, and Liu — still imprisoned — was represented by an empty chair, the first time since the 1936 prize to Carl von Ossietzky, an opponent of the Nazi regime, that no laureate or relative could accept in person.

## Significance

Mario Vargas Llosa's 2010 Nobel Prize represented a milestone for Latin American literature and for Peruvian culture internationally. His fiction explored the intersections of power, sexuality, ideology, and individual liberty within societies shaped by colonialism, caudillismo (strongman rule), and revolution — themes that resonated with both literary critics and general readers. The Swedish Academy's citation specifically acknowledged his ability to map not just political systems but their psychological and moral effects on individuals.

The award sparked debates in Latin America about whether a politically conservative author could credibly claim the mantle of a literary tradition associated with the left. Vargas Llosa's own response — that literature's task is truth and complexity, not political alignment — echoed positions he had staked out since the 1970s.

In subsequent years, Vargas Llosa continued to write, publish essays on democracy and culture, and remain a fixture in Spanish intellectual life, earning the Miguel de Cervantes Prize (the "Spanish Nobel") in 2017 and a Spanish peerage as Marquis of Vargas Llosa in 2023, conferred by King Felipe VI.
