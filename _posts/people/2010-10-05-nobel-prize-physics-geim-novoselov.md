**Key figures**: André Geim (University of Manchester), Konstantin Novoselov (University of Manchester), the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences

## Background: Two Physicists, One Lab

André Konstantin Geim was born on October 21, 1958, in Sochi, Russia. He completed his doctoral studies at the Institute of Solid State Physics, Russian Academy of Sciences (Chernogolovka), and subsequently held research positions in the Netherlands — at the University of Nijmegen (later Radboud University) and the High Field Magnet Laboratory — before moving to the University of Manchester in 2001 as a professor of condensed matter physics. He became a Fellow of the Royal Society in 2007 and holds Dutch citizenship; at the time of the Nobel award he did not hold Russian citizenship.

Konstantin Sergeyevich Novoselov was born on August 23, 1974, in Nizhny Tagil, Russia. He first encountered Geim as a doctoral student at Nijmegen in the late 1990s and followed him to Manchester, where he completed a second PhD in 2004 — the same year their landmark graphene paper was published. Novoselov holds both Russian and British citizenship. At 36 years old when the prize was announced, he became the youngest laureate in physics since Brian Josephson won the prize in 1973 at age 33, ending a 37-year gap during which no younger recipient had been recognized.

## The Discovery: From Pencil Graphite to Nobel

Graphene had been theorized as early as the 1940s by physicist Philip Russell Wallace, and the stability of truly two-dimensional crystals was considered thermodynamically impossible at room temperature by the Mermin–Wagner theorem and related arguments. The conventional wisdom held that thermal fluctuations would destroy the long-range order necessary to maintain a freestanding single-atom layer.

Geim and Novoselov's breakthrough arrived through "Friday Night Experiments" — informal, exploratory sessions that Geim had institutionalized in his lab. In 2003–2004, they began systematically cleaving graphite flakes using ordinary adhesive tape (the "micro-mechanical cleavage" or "Scotch-tape technique"), repeatedly peeling the tape against itself to produce progressively thinner fragments. Crucially, they found that transferring these flakes onto silicon dioxide — a 300 nm oxide layer on a silicon wafer — made single-atom-thick layers visible under an optical microscope due to subtle optical interference effects that added just enough contrast.

Their results appeared in *Science* on October 22, 2004, in the paper "Electric Field Effect in Atomically Thin Carbon Films" (volume 306, pages 666–669), co-authored with Novoselov, Geim, and four colleagues. The paper described the isolation of graphene and demonstrated that its charge carriers — electrons — behaved as though effectively massless, exhibiting the quantum Hall effect even at room temperature. The Nobel Prize followed just six years later — an unusually short interval for recognition of recent experimental work, reflecting the exceptional pace at which graphene had transformed condensed-matter physics. ## Summary

On October 5, 2010, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the Nobel Prize in Physics awarded jointly to André Geim and Konstantin Novoselov "for groundbreaking experiments regarding the two-dimensional material graphene." The announcement described graphene as "the thinnest and strongest material ever measured" and highlighted its extraordinary electrical properties, particularly the ability to study quantum electrodynamic phenomena in a benchtop material.

The prize carried a total monetary award of 10 million Swedish kronor (approximately $1.5 million at 2010 exchange rates), split equally between the two laureates. Both were working at the University of Manchester's School of Physics and Astronomy when the announcement was made. Their collaboration there had produced a series of highly cited papers from 2004 onward establishing graphene's electronic, mechanical, and thermal properties, and training a generation of researchers now scattered across global condensed-matter labs.

The 2010 Nobel Prize season was exceptionally rich: announced the same week were the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (Robert Edwards for IVF) and the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Heck, Negishi, and Suzuki for palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling)]({{ '/news/science-technology/nobel-prize-2010-medicine-chemistry/' | relative_url }}), and three days later [Liu Xiaobo]({{ '/news/people/liu-xiaobo/' | relative_url }}) was named the Nobel Peace Prize laureate while imprisoned in China — a week that illustrated the breadth of the Nobel institution's reach across science, human rights, and geopolitics.

## Properties and the Two-Dimensional Revolution

Graphene's extraordinary combination of properties stems from its structure: carbon atoms in sp² hybridization arranged in a planar hexagonal (honeycomb) lattice, one atom thick (approximately 0.335 nm — the interlayer spacing in graphite). Key measured properties:

- **Mechanical strength**: Young's modulus of approximately 1 terapascal (TPa), making it roughly 200 times stronger than structural steel by specific strength. Breaking strength is about 42 N/m.
- **Electrical conductivity**: Electron mobility at room temperature exceeds 15,000 cm²/(V·s) in encapsulated devices, far surpassing silicon (~1,400 cm²/(V·s)), enabling ultrafast transistors. Charge carriers near the Dirac point behave as massless Dirac fermions, a relativistic quantum phenomenon observable at room temperature.
- **Thermal conductivity**: Approximately 5,000 W/(m·K) for freely suspended graphene — among the highest of any known material.
- **Optical transparency**: Absorbs only ~2.3% of white light per layer, making it nearly transparent and useful in touch screens and photovoltaics.
- **Impermeability**: A defect-free graphene membrane is impermeable even to helium atoms, making it an exceptional barrier material.

Geim and Novoselov's 2004 isolation opened the door to what became the field of two-dimensional materials more broadly: molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), boron nitride, and dozens of other single-layer compounds were subsequently characterized, leading to an entirely new research domain — sometimes called the "graphene family" or "van der Waals heterostructures" — in which single-atom layers of different materials are stacked like atomic-scale Lego bricks to engineer custom electronic, optical, and magnetic properties.

## Significance

The 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics for graphene was significant for the laureates personally, for the scientific field, and for public understanding of research.

**For condensed-matter physics**: The prize signaled the field's transformation away from exclusively bulk materials toward nanoscale and reduced-dimensional systems. Graphene demonstrated that materials could be engineered atom by atom, and that exotic quantum phenomena previously confined to ultra-low-temperature laboratory settings could be observed at room temperature on a tabletop. The discovery validated an entire generation of theorists who had predicted unusual properties of two-dimensional carbon and opened both experimental and theoretical frontiers.

**For science policy and investment**: The Nobel recognition catalyzed a surge of government and industrial investment. The European Commission's Graphene Flagship, announced in 2013 as one of the EU's two Future and Emerging Technologies Flagship initiatives, committed €1 billion over ten years — one of the largest research investments in European history — to translate graphene science into applications in flexible electronics, energy, composites, and biomedical devices. National programs in South Korea, China, the United Kingdom, and the United States followed within three years of the prize.

**For scientific culture**: Geim's achievement — holding both a Nobel Prize (2010) and an Ig Nobel Prize (2000, for magnetically levitating a live frog) — became a widely circulated symbol of the value of curiosity-driven experimentation without predetermined applications. The Scotch-tape origin story was repeated in hundreds of public science communications as evidence that world-changing discoveries can arise from low-cost, playful investigation rather than expensive, narrowly targeted research programs.

**In the Nobel season's context**: Coming in the same week as the [physics Nobel's science counterparts]({{ '/news/science-technology/nobel-prize-2010-medicine-chemistry/' | relative_url }}) and the politically charged [Liu Xiaobo Peace Prize]({{ '/news/people/liu-xiaobo/' | relative_url }}), the graphene announcement received extensive coverage that helped propel the material into mainstream public consciousness — an unusual degree of press attention for a materials science award.
